## What to Do When Someone Hits Your Parked Car and Leaves

A parked-car hit-and-run can leave you with damage and no driver to bill. Start by making the scene safe, recording the damage, and looking for evidence before it disappears. Then report the crash and review your insurance options.

> [!IMPORTANT] Do not drive the car if a tire rubs, fluid leaks, glass blocks your view, or a light or safety system does not work. Arrange a tow instead.

## Immediate Steps at the Scene

Take these steps in order:

1. **Check for urgent hazards.** Move away from traffic, broken glass, leaking fluid, or a damaged electric-vehicle battery.
2. **Do not move the car yet.** First take wide photos that show its position, nearby signs, lane markings, and surrounding businesses or homes.
3. **Record all damage.** Photograph every damaged panel, debris, paint transfer, tire marks, and any note left on the vehicle.
4. **Write down the time window.** Use receipts, parking apps, work records, or doorbell alerts to estimate when the crash happened.
5. **Look for witnesses and cameras.** Ask nearby people and property owners before video is overwritten.
6. **Call the police or use the local online reporting system.** Get a report or incident number.
7. **Notify your insurer.** Report facts only. Avoid guessing about the driver, speed, or cost of repairs.

If someone left a note, preserve the original and photograph it. Verify the name, phone number, plate, and insurance information before treating it as complete.

## How to Find Evidence

Evidence often determines whether police or an insurer can identify the other driver. Search the area promptly because many camera systems erase recordings within days.

### Check Nearby Cameras

Look for:

- Parking-lot and garage cameras
- Storefront security cameras
- Doorbell cameras
- Traffic or transit cameras
- Dashcams in nearby parked vehicles
- Building access or gate records

Ask the owner to preserve footage for the full possible time window. They may release it only to police or an insurer. Give the responding officer the camera location and the contact person's name.

### Document Vehicle Clues

Photograph paint transfer and broken parts without cleaning or moving them. A mirror cover, light lens, trim piece, or paint color can help identify a vehicle type. Do not post a suspected driver's private information online. Give evidence to police and your insurer.

### Ask Witnesses for Specific Details

A useful witness statement may include:

- Plate number or partial plate
- Vehicle make, model, color, or body type
- Direction of travel
- Driver description
- Time of impact
- Photos or video

Get the witness's name and contact information. Write down what the witness said in their own words.

## Should You File a Police Report?

Yes. A police report creates a dated record and may be required for uninsured-motorist claims. Rules and reporting deadlines vary by state and city. If officers do not respond to private-property crashes, use the agency's online or counter-report process.

Include:

- Where the car was parked
- The best estimate of the time window
- Photos and physical evidence
- Witness details
- Camera locations
- Any note or contact information left behind

Keep the report number. A report documents the event, but it does not guarantee that police will identify the driver or decide insurance liability.

## Which Insurance Coverage May Pay?

The answer depends on your policy and state law. Read the declarations page and ask the adjuster which coverage applies.

### Collision Coverage

Collision coverage usually pays for accidental damage to your car even when the other driver is unknown. Your deductible normally applies. If the insurer later recovers money from the driver, it may return some or all of your deductible through subrogation.

### Uninsured Motorist Property Damage

Uninsured motorist property damage, often called UMPD, may cover a hit-and-run in some states and policies. Other states require contact with the other vehicle, identification of the driver, a prompt police report, or corroborating evidence. UMPD can have a deductible or coverage limit.

### Comprehensive Coverage

Comprehensive coverage usually does **not** cover a collision with another vehicle. It commonly covers events such as theft, vandalism, falling objects, weather, fire, or an animal strike. Damage that looks intentional may raise a vandalism question, but the insurer decides from the facts and policy terms.

### The Other Driver's Liability Coverage

If police identify the driver, you may make a property-damage claim against that driver's insurer. The insurer will investigate coverage and fault. Give it photos, the police report, repair estimates, witness information, and camera evidence.

> [!TIP] Ask your insurer to explain in writing which coverage applies, the deductible, rental-car coverage, and whether a claim could affect your policy. Do not delay a required report while waiting to find the driver.

## Is It Worth Making a Claim?

Get a repair estimate before deciding when the car is safe to drive. Visible damage can hide broken brackets, sensors, wiring, or structural parts.

Compare:

- The full repair estimate
- Your deductible
- Rental or towing costs
- Possible calibration costs for cameras and sensors
- Whether the policy requires prompt notice
- Your ability to pay if hidden damage appears later

If repairs cost only slightly more than the deductible, paying directly may be practical. Still document the incident and comply with reporting terms. If the damage is substantial, involves safety equipment, or may hide structural damage, opening a claim can protect your options.

## If the Hit-and-Run Happened in a Parking Lot

A parking lot is private property, but traffic and insurance rules can still apply. Property owners may have video or an incident log. Ask management to preserve evidence and create a written report.

Do not assume the property owner must pay. A lot owner is not usually responsible only because the crash happened there. Liability may be different if unsafe property conditions contributed to the event.

## If the Car Was Parked at Home

Check doorbell footage, home cameras, neighbors' cameras, and delivery records. Save original video files rather than screen recordings when possible. Note whether the car was on a public street, in a shared lot, or on private property because the correct police reporting process may differ.

## If You Find the Driver Later

Give new evidence to police and your insurer. Do not confront the person or accept an informal payment before you know the full repair cost. Hidden damage and rental needs can make an early cash amount inadequate.

If you already used collision coverage, your insurer may pursue the driver. Ask how recovery could affect your deductible. Keep all messages and do not sign a release without understanding what claims it ends.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Washing off paint transfer before photographing it
- Waiting too long to request camera footage
- Repairing the car before the insurer inspects it
- Assuming comprehensive coverage applies
- Guessing about how the crash happened
- Posting accusations or private information online
- Accepting cash before getting a complete repair estimate
- Missing a policy or state reporting deadline

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will insurance cover a parked-car hit-and-run?

Collision coverage often pays subject to your deductible. Uninsured motorist property damage may apply in some states and policies. Coverage depends on your policy language, state law, reporting, and available evidence.

### Do I have to pay a deductible if my parked car was hit?

A collision deductible usually applies when you use your own collision coverage. UMPD may have a different deductible. If the other driver is found, your insurer may try to recover the payment and return some or all of your deductible.

### Can I make a claim without a police report?

You may be able to, but some policies or uninsured-motorist rules require prompt police reporting. A report also supports the time, place, and facts of the loss. Contact the correct police agency and your insurer promptly.

### Will a parked-car hit-and-run claim raise my insurance rate?

Rate rules vary by state and insurer. Ask your insurer how a not-at-fault or hit-and-run claim is classified. No one can promise that a claim will never affect price or renewal.

### What if there is no camera footage?

You can still report the crash and provide photos, paint transfer, debris, witnesses, parking records, and a time window. Your own collision coverage may apply even if the other driver is never identified.

### How long do I have to report the hit-and-run?

Deadlines vary by policy, state, and police agency. Some uninsured-motorist provisions require prompt notice. Report the event as soon as practical rather than relying on a general deadline.
